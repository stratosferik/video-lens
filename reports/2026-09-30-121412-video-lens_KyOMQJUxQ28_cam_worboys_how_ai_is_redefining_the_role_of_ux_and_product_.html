Summary

Cam Worboys, Head of Product Design at Cash App, argues that when AI makes almost anything buildable in minutes, the question is no longer what we can make but what we should make. Designers move from making to choosing: create fast, edit slow, and lead with taste and a bold point of view instead of committees and data-driven hedging. His stance is openly opinionated — in a flood of competent, probabilistic sameness, the real risk is not failure but irrelevance.

#DesignLeadership #AIandDesign #HatchConference2025
In the age of AI and infinite creation, what truly matters is not what can be made but what should be made.

In this keynote from Hatch Conference, Cam Worboys (Head of Product Design, Cash App) explores how the role of the designer is shifting from making to curating. With AI enabling anyone to generate logos, apps, websites, and prototypes in minutes, abundance is no longer the problem. The challenge is discernment, taste, and vision.

Cam draws parallels from history — the flood of posters in 19th-century Paris, playlists that tamed music’s endless catalogs, and outliers like Fantasia — to show why curation, opinion, and bold human vision are now the designer’s greatest advantage.

He argues that in a world of sameness, taste is strategy, editing is design, and opinionated work is the only way to stand out.

Takeaway

Treat reviewing and deleting as first-class design work: after a burst of AI-assisted exploration, deliberately cut the obvious, then amplify the few small, unexpected moments with real power until they become the through-line. If you skip that curation step, you ship the model's statistical middle — polished, fluent, and indistinguishable from everyone else's.

Key Points

  • Abundance creates a demand for discernment — every leap in cheap production has been followed by a new form of curation, and AI is doing the same to software design.

    Chromolithography flooded 1880s Paris with so many posters that residents complained of clutter; the city did not shut the presses but introduced the Morris column to give attention a rhythm. Streaming did the same to music until playlists made endless catalogues navigable. Worboys frames AI as the next instance: as the making gets easier, the output explodes, and the scarce skill becomes selection.

  • The designer's role shifts from making to choosing — as tools automate pixels, flows and specs, the real value becomes judgment.

    He looks to world-class curators as the model: people who select, organise and give meaning to choice. Producing every detail used to be the job; now it is showing what is worth paying attention to. He calls this out explicitly as an existential shift, not a tooling tweak.

  • Create fast, edit slow — embrace AI velocity for exploration, but put the care into editing, because careful editing is curation.

    On Cash App's Arcade design system refresh, the team skips alignment documents and just builds: prompting 10 options of a 3D illustration in 10 minutes, or sketching data-viz ideas in Figma and vibe-coding three functional prototypes in a few hours. He describes this as a multi-software, nonlinear, almost a hacker approach. The result is a pile of mostly mediocre output with a few exceptional kernels buried in it — which is exactly why the editing phase matters.

  • A three-step curation method: delete, amplify, narrate — cut the obvious and redundant, pull forward the ideas with legs, then tie them into a story.

    Deleting is the hardest part; sometimes it is easy (off-brand, customers don't love it), sometimes it requires answering abstract questions like the company's philosophy on the future of payments. Amplifying means spotting small, random moments during the build — a bold use of green, togetherness shown as a cluster of icons, generous white space, a single icon — and pushing them until they become the through-line. He calls spotting those moments the most important skill in a build-first process.

  • Taste is strategy; get off the fence — as barriers to creation fall, taste is the differentiator, and otherwise, a computer will be better than you.

    Worboys says design and tech lost the habit of leading with instinct: teams outsourced decisions to data because it meant nobody got fired, and accepted design by committee. He cites Rick Rubin, who says he is paid for being decisive about what he likes and confident in his taste. Reviewing work should be owned as being as important as making it.

  • AI has a house style — the probabilistic middle — models return the statistically likely answer, so first drafts are fluent, polished and eerily similar.

    He places AI in a lineage of tools that shaped their era's aesthetic: Flash and Photoshop brought skeuomorphism and glossy buttons, iOS 7 and Sketch brought flat UI and background blurs, and design systems brought identical systems with slightly different content. AI's influence may be even stronger, because middle kind of means boring. Sameness is becoming the new noise.

  • The outlier wins through a human vision the technology enables — Fantasia broke out of a sea of similar 1930s comedy shorts.

    Blending classical music, abstract storytelling and new immersive sound, it came from a team determined to redefine what animation could be. That, he argues, is the human opportunity with AI: push beyond the prompt, ask harder questions, and inject a point of view, because the greatest risk when building is so easy is not failure, it's irrelevance.

  • Cash App's opinionated design, even when it breaks "good design" rules — the vision to redefine the world's relationship with money filters into every product decision.

    Examples: fully custom debit cards (dark glitter, black metal, hot pink, the new tortoiseshell pattern) with a matching app theme; a payment flow that skips the native keyboard and may even take more taps, but expresses simplicity and focus; personalised payment assets that make a transaction expressive; and physical spaces with music and artists instead of beige branches with iPads. He ties it to results — Cash App has moved over a trillion dollars — to argue that opinionated design is not designers just having fun.
